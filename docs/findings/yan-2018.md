## The reported sentence

Page 18 of the PDF (printed page "18 / 24", PDF index 17), Experiment 4, Results:

> A chi-square test examined the effect of language switching on food choice. We combined the English- and Chinese-language groups into one non-switching group, and compared the results to those of the switching group. The effect of mindset switching was significant, χ2(1, N = 89) = 5.00, p = 0.042. [...] In the non-switching groups, 51.79% of the participants selected the M&M chocolates as their snack. The ratio of M&Ms increased to 75.76% in the language-switching group.

Flag that triggered this check: a Pearson chi-square of 5.00 on 1 df has p = 0.0253, not 0.042.

Context from the other two chi-square tests in the same paper, recomputed the same way from S1 Data: Experiment 1 (p. 7) reports chi2(1, N = 91) = 4.44, p = 0.035; the raw data (Dummy1 by WantShouldChoice, table [[33, 8], [30, 20]]) give Pearson 4.439, p = 0.0351, Fisher p = 0.0419. Experiment 3 (p. 14) reports chi2(1, N = 107) = 7.25, p = 0.007; the raw data ([[35, 2], [51, 19]]) give Pearson 7.251, p = 0.0071, Fisher p = 0.0092. In both of those the reported p is the Pearson p. Only in Experiment 4 does the reported p match Fisher's exact test rather than the Pearson statistic that sits next to it. (Side note: in sheet Exp1 the header text for `SwitchGroup` says switching = 1, but the Dummy1 column and the paper's group sizes show switching = 3; Dummy1 was used.)

## Plausible benign explanations

- Mixed rows from one software output. SPSS Crosstabs prints Pearson chi-square, continuity correction, likelihood ratio and Fisher's exact test in one block. Reading the statistic from the Pearson row and the p from the Fisher row gives exactly "5.00, p = 0.042". This fits the numbers best, because 0.0423 rounds to 0.042 while the Yates p rounds to 0.044.
- Continuity correction. The Yates-corrected p (0.0443) is close to, but does not round to, 0.042.
- Fisher's exact test reported as a chi-square. The authors may have intended to report the exact test and labelled it with the chi-square notation and the Pearson statistic.
- A typo in either number. There is no 1-df chi-square in the output that gives p = 0.042 (that would need 4.135), and the Pearson statistic 5.00 is confirmed by the data, so a typo in the p (for example 0.025 mistyped) is possible but the Fisher explanation needs no typo.
- A different N or grouping. Ruled out: N = 89, the percentages and the pooled grouping all reproduce exactly from S1 Data, and the three-group test gives neither value.
- The p from a different model. The ANOVA two lines later reports F(2, 86) = 5.00, p = 0.009, which shares the "5.00" but not the p, so a copy from that line does not explain 0.042 either.

## What it would take to settle it

The raw data are already public (S1 Data) and reproduce the table exactly, so the remaining unknown is only which procedure produced the p. The analysis script or the software output (for example the SPSS Crosstabs table, or the R call) for Experiment 4 would settle whether 0.042 is Fisher's exact p placed next to the Pearson statistic, or something else. A one-line correction from the authors or an erratum would resolve the mismatch in the printed text.

## Note: reported vs recomputed

This is a numerical discrepancy in the reporting of one test. The reported statistic, χ2(1, N = 89) = 5.00, reproduces exactly from the published data (5.0007 without continuity correction), but its p-value is 0.0253, not the reported 0.042. The reported 0.042 matches, to the printed precision, Fisher's exact two-sided test on the same table (0.0423). Under the uncorrected statistic, under the Yates correction (p = 0.044) and under Fisher's exact test (p = 0.042), the result is significant at the .05 level, so the significance conclusion drawn in the paper is unchanged whichever of these is taken as the intended test. The percentages, the group sizes and the direction of the effect all match the raw data. No inference about the authors' conduct is made or implied; the pattern is consistent with pairing a chi-square statistic with the p-value from an adjacent row of the same output table.
